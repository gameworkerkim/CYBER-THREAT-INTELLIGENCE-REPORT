# DPRK Arrests Homegrown Hackers Accused of Hitting Its Own Central Banks

> **Report ID** `CTI-2026-0726-DPRK-BANK-HACKERS` | **Published** 2026-07-26 | **Classification** `TLP:GREEN` | **Severity** MEDIUM  
> **Author** Dennis Kim (HoKwang Kim) | <gameworker@gmail.com> | [@gameworkerkim](https://github.com/gameworkerkim)  
> **Caution** This OSINT brief reconstructs open reporting based on Daily NK anonymous sources and is **not independently verified**.

---

## Opening

It is well established that North Korea runs highly active hacking campaigns for profit and disruption—including against crypto targets and victims in South Korea and Japan. Reporting now claims that the same spear turned inward: elite hackers trained by the regime allegedly compromised the country’s Central Bank and Foreign Trade Bank, and authorities arrested them. This brief reconstructs the narrative for cyber-security, sanctions, and internal-control audiences.

---

## 1. Incident overview — who, what, how?

On **12 July 2026**, state security officers allegedly conducted an emergency arrest at a safehouse in Pyongyang. Those detained were described as an elite hacker group cultivated by the regime.

Citing anonymous Pyongyang sources, South Korean outlet **Daily NK** reported that the operators penetrated the internal networks of the **Central Bank of the DPRK** and the **Foreign Trade Bank**—respectively responsible for issuance/management of state funds and for foreign-exchange and trade settlement.

The core cadre is described as former personnel from a **military cyber intelligence unit**, expanded by recruiting strong IT talent from **Kim Chaek University of Technology** and **Pyongyang University of Science and Technology (PUST)**. Reporting mentions military-grade hacking techniques, encrypted messengers such as Telegram, and Chinese wireless equipment.

### Laundering path — Chinese brokers and border cash

Stolen funds were allegedly moved to overseas crypto wallets, cashed out to USD/CNY via **Chinese brokers**, then ferried through border cities such as **Sinuiju** and **Hyesan** back into the North. Split remittances, encrypted messengers, and unregistered phones are cited as evasion tactics.

---

## 2. How were they discovered? — traces of mismatch

Authorities supposedly flagged **inconsistencies in foreign-currency settlement approvals** and **suspicious foreign IP access**. The narrative places this in a dual audit culture—online logs plus manual/paper verification—shaped by constant external hacking pressure. Encrypted crypto-related traffic was traced to a Pyongyang safehouse; the raid reportedly seized hundreds of thousands of dollars’ worth of equipment and phones registered under false identities.

Afterward, armed guards were said to have been posted at both banks, with RF intercept vehicles deployed across Pyongyang.

---

## 3. Context — the “$2 billion” confusion and internal fracture

Headlines about **“$2 billion hackers”** usually do **not** describe the loss from this single incident. Per **Chainalysis**, DPRK-linked actors stole roughly **$2 billion in crypto worldwide in 2025**; **TRM Labs** estimated that by April 2026 such actors accounted for about **76%** of global crypto hack/fraud losses. **The amount taken in this alleged internal bank case remains unknown.**

Even so, the implication is large. Unlike overseas targeting associated with Kimsuky, Lazarus, and historical DeFi heists (e.g., Ronin ~$620M, Harmony ~$100M), this story—if accurate—describes **regime-trained elites looting domestic treasuries**, a signal of control fracture.

Daily NK-quoted source:

> *"They learned the skills to protect the state, but plundered the national treasury."*

Harsh sentencing is widely expected in such a scenario.

---

## 4. Limits and implications — unverified, still a meaningful signal

This account depends on Daily NK’s anonymous sourcing and **has not been independently verified**. Confirming internal DPRK news is chronically hard under information control. Secondary observations sometimes cited—partial internet disruption for about a week from ~12 July, and a sharp drop in outbound DPRK hacking attempts—can at best be treated as **corroborating signals**, not proof.

The episode nonetheless asks whether the Achilles’ heel of one of the world’s most active state hacking apparatuses sits **inside** the control system. If the spear now points at the master, it becomes a barometer of internal cracks.
